Why Use Figures?

Data visualisations are important:

  • For the analyst, to understand what is going on in the data (errors, unexpected values, analyses to run)
  • For the reader, to access the message and be convinced: a report tells a story, and each figure supports it

Figures can also lie or mislead: use them with ethics and professionalism.

Zoom on Median and Quartiles

Sort the values, then cut them into four groups of equal size. The interquartile range (IQR = Q3 - Q1) holds the middle 50% of the data.

Zoom on the Box Plot

  • The box runs from Q1 to Q3, and the line inside is the median
  • The whiskers stop at the most extreme values within 1.5 × IQR of the box
  • Anything further away is drawn as a dot: a possible outlier

Zoom on Mean and Standard Deviation

  • Mean (or average) is the sum of all the values divided by their number
  • Standard deviation (SD) is the typical distance to the mean: the square root of the average squared distance

Source: Cuemath

Zoom on Regression

The line that best fits the points of two continuous variables is a regression line: Y = b₀ + b₁X + e

  • b₀ is the intercept: the value of Y when X is 0
  • b₁ is the slope: how much Y changes when X increases by 1
  • e is the error (or residual): the distance between each point and the line

Zoom on Correlation

The correlation (r) is the slope of the regression line once both variables are on the same scale. It measures how closely the points follow a straight line, from -1 to +1:

3. The Key Figures in Tableau

Variable Types and Tableau Figures

A family of figures for each type of variable:

  • Composition: 1 Dimension
  • Distribution: 1 Measure
  • Comparison: 2 Dimensions, or 1 Dimension and 1 Measure
  • Relationship: 2 Measures

Show Me highlights the figures they allow, it also explicitely indicate what combinaison of Dimension and Measure is needed.

Composition in Tableau

A Dimension on Columns and the count field on Rows. For percentages, open the menu of the count pill (click its arrow, or right-click / ⌃ Control + click) → Quick Table Calculation → Percent of Total.

The recordings in this section use another dataset: the clicks are the same with insta-data.csv.

Composition in Tableau: Pie Chart

Only for variables with two categories: the Dimension on Columns, the count on Rows, then Show Me → pie chart.

Distribution in Tableau

Tableau does not draw every distribution figure:

  • Histogram: yes, with Show Me
  • Density: no density curve out of the box
  • Box plot: yes, with Show Me or the Analytics pane
  • Dynamite: an average bar, but no error bars

Distribution in Tableau: Histogram

  • A Measure on Rows, then Show Me → histogram.

  • Tableau creates a new field, variable (bin): to change the width of the bars, open its menu → Edit.

Distribution in Tableau: Box Plot

  • A Measure on Rows, then Show Me → box-plot
  • Use dimension to display all the values and the 2 whiskers.

Distribution in Tableau: Average Bar

  • A Measure on Rows is a SUM by default: open its menu → Measure → Average.
  • Alternatively, the same menu offers Median if needed.

Comparison in Tableau

Add a Dimension on Colour, Columns or Rows to any of the previous figures: bar or pie charts with the count, histograms, box plots, bar charts with the average.

Comparison in Tableau: Multiple Bars

A second Dimension on Columns puts the bars side by side; on Colour, it tells the groups apart.

Comparing Distributions in Tableau

A Dimension on Rows: one histogram per group

A Dimension on Columns: one box per group

Relationship in Tableau

The scatterplot is the best way to show the relationship between 2 Measures, but:

  • switch both from the default SUM to Dimension (pill menu), or Tableau draws a single dot for the sum of everything
  • add a Trend Line from the Analytics pane

Relationship in Tableau: Scatterplot

Two Measures on Columns and Rows, each switched to Dimension. Analytics pane → Trend Line → Linear. A Dimension on Colour gives one line per group: hover a line to read its R² and p-value.
